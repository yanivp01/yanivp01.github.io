The Next Great Teachers May Be the Workers AI Displaces

  • Report this article
Yaniv P.

Yaniv P.

Published Jul 22, 2026
+ Follow

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=eU-MMBDdMUI

I just saw this remarkable video. In the USA, with similar trends across much of the west, girls and women, "outperform" boys and men, in education. This is unprecedented in history, and we need to start thinking about what issues may exist in educational systems that lead to this imbalance.

Obviously, this is only applicable to the western world, and particularly America, and I can't speak beyond my own experiences, none of which involve American education, nor UK primary or secondary education in the modern age. However, I can speak with some confidence on adult and non-institutional education, and while I am by far no world leader, I am not uneducated on the theory of the future of work. This however is purely my musings.

First and foremost, this video made me think on the structure of school itself. I think that, "those who can't do, teach", is true. Think - people are rewarded more when they make a clearer, more immediate contribution to society - teaching is by far the most complex, slowest to impact action a human can do, through no fault of the discipline.

The compounded technical, communicational, organisational, and social skills required to make a good teacher are almost always each rewarded better in fields that make something happen rather than teach kids, and this is multiplicative when combined, meaning that while the best coder will probably code, the best teacher probably runs a company. You'll get examples of such people delivering high-quality education momentarily, ad hoc, outside the school system, and for massive sums, but not within it for the normal person.

To this end, I feel a genuine solution presents itself with AI in the workforce. Pretty much anyone can learn the technicalities of brush techniques or atomic spin states without someone there to guide them - we can ask ChatGPT for an intro to each, some references, and then start googling our own resources. The challenge is the realisation that one must do this, overcoming the noise of facts with an appropriate mental filtering model, and securing unique knowledge that other people don't have. This needs people to empower each person uniquely. Despite this, education (and support work, actually) has a massive gap in staffing, needing more and better teachers delivering more of the right kind of education for the right kind of person.

By contrast, day by day, we are reducing the relative value of low-mid level technical know-how in numerous subjects, but increasing the value of high level technical know-how, novel datasets, and social networks. This means that the number of people who can't do is increasing despite, technically, them becoming more able. It also means that the benefits of becoming someone who can do are also increasing, so it is worth more to keep working on a longer path to get there. Being an educator makes for a clear step on this path, since it gives people a space to be rewarded for studying further as they learn, while performing complex, useful tasks themselves. It will also have the effect of improving the technical competency of the teaching populace and opening up the space for teaching specialists to pioneer new pedagogical models to start enabling more students to walk this long, diverse path themselves. To put it simply, this glut of people who are highly skilled, but are being outperformed by AI tools for their roles - those who cannot do - can solve these combined problems through becoming educators - teach.

Higher level experts need to become teachers on their own terms, and teaching must evolve to accommodate this new resource and the world that generated it, so as to help us start generating more exceptional students.

Key to this is realigning education along cognitive process rather than subject. The scientific, engineering, communicative, mathematical, and innovative processes, for example, are each wholly distinct methods of thinking and truth discovery, where truth even has a distinct definition.

The barriers between, for example, biology, chemistry, and physics, need to be dismantled and replaced with the broader topic of scientific thought, with cases of its application in the sciences. Maths should be integrated with logic and rhetoric, maybe even theology, geared around the basic mathematical process. Art, drama, literature should focus on the communicative process. The list goes on. This is a structurally more complex model, facilitated by the greater time that teachers would have while technical/ case experts bolster sessions, allowing these teaching practitioners to focus further on theory of pedagogy. This should be built alongside these external specialists, bridging the gap with AI tools to achieve these higher results with the same workload. The skills of the professional teacher would be in partnership with specialist professionals to design courses that frame the unique knowledge this external professional holds within a broader curriculum, leaning further, in a sense, to being a consultant and administrator. I believe strongly that such a model is far better suited to the diverse thought processes of a classroom of unique, individual children, and can become an important pillar in the growth of adults as well.

It's nice when a video makes me think this hard, I won't lie, and strongly recommend it. But this piece here I'm writing is just my thoughts - I would appreciate opinions, counter-arguments, counter-evidence, details supporting this, and information on who is working towards exactly this. Thank you very much!
